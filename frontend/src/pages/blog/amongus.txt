# the infinite among us pattern that stole 8 months of my life

= introduction
tiling (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessellation) is a topic that has
absolutely enchanted me, and if youre as big a fan of both math and art as i
am, then maybe you'll be enchanted too. we humans for all of history have
covered our walls, our streets, and our places of worship with tiles, and it is
modern mathematics that allows hobbiests like me to play around with and
discover new, novel tilings.

in fact, the tiling of today's focus, what i have dubbed *the among us tiling*,
seems to have been discovered independently by several hobbiests since the
popularization of among us in late 2020. 

the among us tiling has been a great vehicle for bettering my own mathematical
abilities. since, in general, i aim to solve the following question:

"for a pixel rendering of the among us tiling, how can you determine the
color of any given pixel (i, j)?"

= procedural generation
the first thing you may notice about the among us tiling is that it is
entirely composed of [ (pronounced 'crew'), where half are right side up and
the rest are ] (pronounced 'mate'). moreover, each [ has a corresponding ]
joined at the legs. with this we can instead treat both tiles together as a
single *translational unit*. { some diagram showing [, ], and translational unit }
lets refer to the translational unit, the union of [ and ], as a "crewmate."

== an algorithm

take a crewmate. now place another one atop it so that the first crewmate's [
touches the ] of the other crewmate. repeating this will form infitite strips,
and those strips can be placed snugly next to one another. congratuations, you
just tiled the plane!

this algorithm is so simple that it can be verified mentally just by looking at
a picture of the among us tiling. { see also conway criterion }

== note
this algorithm is incredibly simple, and i feel it would be fairly easy to
implement as a computer program, but unfortunately it is not what i am after.
recall the question we are trying to answer: *how can
you determine the color of any given pixel (i, j) of the among us tiling?*

it's a subtle difference, but requires a much different approach than our
procedural algorithm from earlier. rather than starting with [, rotating a copy
for ], joining them together to form a crewmate, joining those into strips, and
joining those strips into a completely tiling, i instead want a *function*. if
you were writing this function in a C-like language you could imagine a function
signature like:

`Color amongUsTiling(int x, int y);`

which takes in a pixel coordinate and returns a color, exactly what i am after!

... but why? why introduce this pointless mathematical abstraction when we already
have a perfectly working and admittedly quite elegant procedural algorithm?
